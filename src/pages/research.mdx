# Research

<div className="lead">
How do independently-developed digital twins become an ecosystem that software agents can
actually reason about? That question was the subject of my Ph.D. thesis, and I keep pulling
on the thread.
</div>

## Digital Twin Ecosystems

Digital twins are usually built one at a time, for one system, by one team. The moment you want
two of them to cooperate, you discover that neither can describe itself to the other.

I work on hypermedia and Linked Data as the interoperability layer: **self-describing digital
twins** that publish their own affordances, so a client — human or agent — can discover what a
twin represents and what it can do at runtime rather than at integration time. This runs from the
modelling side ([Linked Data for ecosystems of digital twins](https://doi.org/10.1145/3652620.3687782))
through to toolchains ([HWoDT](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.softx.2025.102277)) and out to the
edge-cloud continuum.

## Engineering Multi-Agent Systems

The BDI model gives agents a vocabulary for beliefs, desires and intentions — but the frameworks
that implement it have real engineering gaps. I've worked on their **concurrency models**, on
**explainability** (both for the engineer debugging an agent and for one agent explaining itself
to another), on **anticipatory reasoning**, and on JaKtA, a BDI language embedded in pure Kotlin.

## Web of Things & Hypermedia MAS

If the environment of an agent is the Web, then hypermedia controls are its affordances. I'm
interested in how agents discover and use them, how domain experts — not programmers — can
configure industrial hypermedia MAS, and what **embodiment** means for an agent in a hypermedia
space.

## Applications

The abstractions get tested against real domains: operating-room management and oncologic
pharmaceutical supply chains in healthcare, smart manufacturing and Industry 4.0, and the urban
digital twin of the city of Bologna.
